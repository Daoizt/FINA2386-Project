# Speaker 4 · Who gets the second microphone?

About two minutes. Start with the paired network, then reveal the permutation and popularity figures.

Ed Sheeran has a second microphone. On *I Don’t Care*, he hands it to Justin Bieber. On *Best Part of Me*, he hands it to Yebba. Same singer. Two duets. Under our follower cutoff, only the first puts two superstars on the same track.

Now imagine all the superstar collaborations in our data. For every hundred partner credits, how many would you expect to go to another superstar?

[Pause before revealing the benchmark.]

We kept every song, every partnership, and every repeat collaboration in place. Then we shuffled the superstar labels among artists with similar numbers of partners. We did that nearly five thousand times. The benchmark was about **36 out of 100**. The real number? **56**. That’s twenty extra superstar partners per hundred credits, even after accounting for how connected these artists are.

But who gets the microphone is only half the story. What happens to the song?

Across more than four thousand collaborations, all-superstar tracks scored about **eight points higher** on Spotify, after we accounted for release year and the number of artists. Here’s the twist. If we only look at tracks that already scored 65 or higher, the gap shrinks to just over **two points**.

So the answer changes with the playlist you open. Look across all releases and the superstar gap is sizeable. Open only the hits and most of that gap disappears. We can see who shared the microphone and how their songs scored. What we can’t hear in the data is how the very same song would have sounded, or scored, with someone else.

That leaves a different question for our next speaker. What happens when an artist connects two circles that rarely meet?
